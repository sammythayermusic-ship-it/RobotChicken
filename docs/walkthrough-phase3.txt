Part two. Where the money comes from.

You launch COCK on pump fun, paired to PUMP, with the creator fee set to three percent. That means every time anyone buys or sells COCK, three percent of that trade comes to you as the creator, and because the coin is paired to PUMP, those fees arrive in PUMP. Not SOL. PUMP.

Seventy percent of every fee goes to players. Thirty percent is yours, for servers, art, marketing, and a reserve. That seventy for players splits again: fifty percent of all fees is the prize pool for winning matches, and twenty percent is the daily pool for the top ten players.

Part three. How a win gets paid.

Here's the rule that makes the whole thing safe. Prizes are never a fixed amount. Every minute, the worker looks at how much was claimed in the last twenty four hours and how many matches were played in that time, and divides one by the other. That number is what the next win pays. If a lot of people are playing, each prize is smaller. If fees go up, each prize goes up. The game can never promise more than fees actually delivered.

Tiers make it bigger for bigger holders. There are three. Rust, at thirty dollars of COCK, which is the minimum to play at all. Chrome, at two hundred fifty dollars. And Gold, at a thousand dollars. A Rust win pays the base prize. A Chrome win pays two and a half times that. A Gold win pays six times that. Everyone draws from the same pool, so a whale can't drain it by grinding: the more Gold matches happen, the more the pool spreads out.

Matchmaking pairs you with your own tier first. If nobody in your tier shows up within sixty seconds, you get matched with anyone, and that fight pays at the lower of the two tiers. So a Gold player can't farm Rust players for Gold-sized money.

For scale: at five hundred thousand dollars of daily trading volume, fees are fifteen thousand dollars a day. With fifteen hundred matches a day, a Rust win pays about three dollars, Chrome about seven, Gold about seventeen, and first place on the daily board takes about seven hundred fifty dollars. At fifty thousand of volume, divide all of that by ten.

The money lands in the winner's wallet within about ten seconds of the knockout, with a link to the transaction on the result screen.

Part four. The daily top ten.

At midnight UTC every day, the worker takes twenty percent of that day's fees, ranks everyone by wins, and pays the top ten. First place gets twenty five percent of the pool, then eighteen, thirteen, ten, eight, seven, six, five, four, and four. The leaderboard page shows the pool growing all day, a countdown, and what each rank would earn right now.

Part five. Your seed, and why it can't run dry.

You said you'd fund the wallet at launch. That seed only does one job: on quiet days, when fees don't cover the minimum prizes you set, it tops them up. It can never drain faster than one fourteenth per day, so no matter what happens, it lasts at least two weeks. And it's only drawn on when it's needed. If fees cover the day, nothing comes out. When the seed is gone, the site just shows the real numbers.

On top of that, the hot wallet, the one that pays players, only ever holds about thirty six hours of payouts. There are caps per payment, per hour and per day; if any of them trips, payouts pause and your Telegram gets a message. And every payout has a unique key, so a crash or a retry can never pay anyone twice. Before I build any wallet code, I build a simulator that runs a fake month, with whale farming, bot bursts and dead days, and proves every one of these rules holds.

Part six. What the site shows.

The front page shows the live pool: what the next win pays in each tier, today's leaderboard pool and countdown, total fees claimed, total paid to players, and how much seed is left. Every number comes straight from the ledger, so nothing on the page can be stale or made up.
